From a standardized contrast to semiparametric inference and a reproducible report

Before reading: Read Preprocessing and the quantity being estimated, From a research question to an identified estimand, and From an influence function to an estimator. The target and data-audit sections can be read before completing all the proofs.

1 One research argument, from measurements to a reported contrast

We will analyze 24 synthetic expression features from independently sampled people. Group membership is associated with a recorded covariate and technical batch. Some whole-assay measurements are missing. The question is: how do the groups differ on the supplied log2 expression scale after standardizing to a common covariate distribution?

This extended worked application follows one argument: define the target, audit the data, construct its estimator and uncertainty, then assess the reported family of claims. Simulation checks specific predictions of that argument. The From an influence function to an estimator page supplies the proof pattern; here we combine group standardization with missing outcomes.

Everything in this example is synthetic. Feature IDs are invented. The generator supplies known truth for evaluation, while the estimator receives only the observed assay and metadata. No clinical or biological finding is being asserted.

2 Define the population, scale, and target

Let A be group (0 or 1), C=(Z,B) the recorded covariate and batch, and Y_g a feature’s calibrated log2 intensity. Define

\begin{aligned} \mu_{ag}(c)&=E[Y_g\mid A=a,C=c],\\ \psi_g&=E_C[\mu_{1g}(C)-\mu_{0g}(C)]. \end{aligned}

The outer distribution is that of all eligible people in the source population, estimated using everyone’s recorded C, including people with missing assays. We do not condition on the realized covariate values as a fixed population. Variation in the sampled covariate mix therefore contributes to the influence function.

Decision Specification in this case
Independent unit One person, one specimen, one assay occasion
Population The source population sampled by the synthetic generator
Measurement Supplied calibrated log2 intensity; no learned normalization
Contrast Mean conditional group difference standardized over the source population’s C distribution
Missingness One recorded indicator R for a missing whole assay; all covariates remain observed
Family All 24 prespecified feature hypotheses H_{0g}:\psi_g=0
Interpretation A standardized association; no causal interpretation is assumed

A log2 contrast exponentiates to a ratio of geometric means of the two standardized intensity distributions. It is not a ratio of arithmetic mean expression or an absolute molecules-per-cell contrast. The Preprocessing and the quantity being estimated page explains those distinctions. Real count data would need their own measurement and normalization argument before this analysis could be reused.

Group contrasts can vary with C. We do not impose a constant coefficient or a common regression slope across groups. Nor does adjustment alone turn this association into an intervention effect. A causal interpretation would need a well-defined intervention and additional identification assumptions.

3 Reconstruct the observation process

We observe W=(C,A,R,RY_1,\ldots,RY_G). Algebraically RY_g contributes zero when R=0; the stored assay uses NA for those outcomes. Treating those outcomes as measured zeros would change the analysis.

Assume E[|Y_g|]<\infty, outcome missingness at random given (A,C), and positive group and observation probabilities on the target support:

\begin{aligned} e(c)&=P(A=1\mid C=c),\\ \pi_a(c)&=P(R=1\mid A=a,C=c),\\ q_a(c)&=P(A=a,R=1\mid C=c). \end{aligned}

Thus q_1=e\pi_1 and q_0=(1-e)\pi_0. MAR identifies \mu_{ag} from the observed outcomes within group a. Positive q_a provides data for each required group/covariate combination. Stronger lower bounds will support the simple variance and remainder argument below.

The missingness assumption is known in this generator. In real assays, failure to detect a low-abundance feature can depend on its unobserved value; that mechanism is not automatically MAR. Feature-specific non-detection, missing covariates, repeated specimens, and perfect group–batch confounding each require a revised analysis. See Missingness, selection, and the observed-data target and Units of analysis, replication, and study design.

The assay has features in rows and person IDs in columns. Metadata are intentionally shuffled. AlignExpressionStudy() checks unique IDs, an exact match of specimen sets, and explicit column alignment. It rejects nonfinite observed outcomes and nonmissing values on rows declared unobserved. No complete-case sample exclusion is used to define the covariate distribution. Every person remains in the analysis.

The generator uses Z\sim\operatorname{Uniform}(-1,1) and B\sim\operatorname{Bernoulli}(1/2) independently, with

\begin{aligned} \operatorname{logit}e(C)&=-0.1+Z+0.5B,\\ \operatorname{logit}\pi_A(C)&=0.6-0.6A +0.5Z-0.3B. \end{aligned}

Here \operatorname{logit}(p)=\log\{p/(1-p)\}; applying its inverse expit turns each linear predictor into a probability. This is a convenient factorization of the joint distribution, not a claim that technical batch causes group status. The conditional outcome mean is

\mu_{ag}(Z,B)=b_g+c_gZ+d_g(Z^2-1/3) +t_gB+a(\delta_g+h_gZ).

A shared bounded person-level noise component and independent bounded feature noise induce correlated features. Their conditional means are zero. Since E[Z]=0, the population standardized contrast is \psi_g=\delta_g. The first six features have nonzero \delta_g; the remaining eighteen have zero standardized contrasts, even though their conditional group contrasts vary with Z.

The generator returns these oracle values in a separate truth table. EstimateExpressionContrast() has no truth argument and does not receive the complete missing outcomes.

5 Derive the contribution before fitting a model

The influence function in the unrestricted observed-data model, under identification and square integrability, is

\phi_g(W)=\mu_{1g}(C)-\mu_{0g}(C)-\psi_g +\frac{AR}{q_1(C)}\{Y_g-\mu_{1g}(C)\} -\frac{(1-A)R}{q_0(C)}\{Y_g-\mu_{0g}(C)\}.

The first line captures variation in the population’s covariate mix. The second uses observed group-1 residuals; the third subtracts observed group-0 residuals. Conditional centering gives mean zero. In an inner product with an outcome-law score, the joint observation weight cancels the probability of observing that group’s outcome. Scores changing group assignment or observation alone have zero derivative for this functional. Covariate-distribution scores pair with the first line. This follows the derivation in From an influence function to an estimator separately for the two standardized means.

The uncentered contribution starts with the two predicted means for everyone’s C. An observed group-1 person then adds their weighted prediction error; an observed group-0 person subtracts theirs. Someone with a missing assay contributes only the predicted contrast, but remains part of the target covariate population. For example, suppose a held-out person’s predictions are 6.5 and 6.0. If they are observed in group 1 with outcome 7.0 and fitted joint probability 0.25, their contribution is 0.5+(7.0-6.5)/0.25=2.5. That is a weighted statistical contribution, not their measured or causal individual contrast.

Why the joint probability? Among people with the same C, only a fraction q_1(C) both belong to group 1 and have an observed assay. Multiplying by AR/q_1(C) makes that subgroup’s residual correction represent its conditional population average. Weighting by the group probability alone would leave assay selection uncorrected; weighting by observation alone would leave the unequal group distribution of C uncorrected.

For held-out person i, estimate all nuisances on other people, construct the uncentered contribution \hat D_{ig}=\hat\phi_{ig}+\hat\psi_g, and average it.

NoteAlgorithm: cross-fitted standardized expression contrast

Inputs: the aligned observed assay, all people’s covariates and observation indicators, and prespecified fitting choices.

  1. Randomly assign independent people to five folds. All features for a person stay together.
  2. On each training set, fit group membership using a ~ z + batch and observation using r ~ a + z + batch.
  3. Fit a quadratic outcome regression separately in each group, using only its observed training outcomes. All genes use the same design, but have separate coefficients.
  4. Predict both group means, both observation probabilities, and the group probability on held-out covariates.
  5. Add residual corrections only where the corresponding outcome is observed, then combine all held-out contributions.

Output: one contribution per person and feature, and their feature-wise means. Uncertainty additionally requires the remainder and sampling conditions below.

The residual correction uses q_a, not just the group probability or just the assay observation probability. It never multiplies an NA residual by zero. In code, the group-1 update is:

Code 
observed_1 <- a == 1 & r == 1
score[observed_1, ] <- score[observed_1, , drop = FALSE] +
  sweep(y[observed_1, , drop = FALSE] - m1[observed_1, , drop = FALSE],
        1, q1[observed_1], "/")

This is the same code used in the downloadable function. For repeated specimens, splitting arbitrary columns would leak person-level information and the independent-person SE below would be inappropriate.

6 Name the remainder and the inferential conditions

Hold one training fit fixed. Let \Delta\mu_{ag}=\hat\mu_{ag}-\mu_{ag}. Its exact population bias is

P\hat D_g-\psi_g=E\!\left[\frac{\hat q_1-q_1}{\hat q_1}\Delta\mu_{1g}\right] -E\!\left[\frac{\hat q_0-q_0}{\hat q_0}\Delta\mu_{0g}\right].

If each fitted joint probability is at least a fixed \kappa>0, its magnitude is bounded by

\kappa^{-1}\big\{\|\hat q_1-q_1\|_2\|\Delta\mu_{1g}\|_2 +\|\hat q_0-q_0\|_2\|\Delta\mu_{0g}\|_2\big\}.

Both correct outcome regressions can make this bias vanish with controlled, incorrect weights. Correct joint probabilities can do so with controlled, incorrect outcome regressions. These are consistency statements. They do not automatically justify the same estimated influence-function SE under misspecification.

For the efficient first-order argument used here, require i.i.d. people; fixed fold count with nonvanishing fold proportions; MAR; true and fitted joint probabilities bounded below; bounded outcomes and fitted means; L2 consistency of all nuisances; each displayed product o_p(n^{-1/2}); and finite nonzero influence variance. The fold-wise conditional proof in From an influence function to an estimator controls the empirical remainder. The bias bound controls the other remainder. Then

\begin{aligned} \hat\psi_g-\psi_g&=(P_n-P)\phi_g+o_p(n^{-1/2}),\\ \widehat{\operatorname{SE}}_g&=\frac{\operatorname{SD}(\hat D_{ig}-\hat\psi_g)}{\sqrt n}. \end{aligned}

The default generator and correctly specified finite-dimensional fits provide a transparent setting for these conditions. This does not demonstrate that those nuisance models are adequate in a real cohort. Nor does the fixed-24-feature argument establish uniform validity when the number of features grows rapidly with sample size.

The SE formula uses variation in the constructed contributions across all sampled people, including those with missing assays. It is not the raw expression SD divided by the square root of the observed assay count. Cross-fitting contributions share training fits; their dependence becomes negligible at first order only through the displayed expansion. The proof, rather than treating fitted scores as new independent data, justifies this variance calculation.

8 Address the family of claims

Use the two-sided asymptotic Wald p-value for H_{0g}:\psi_g=0. All 24 tests belong to the prespecified family. Shared noise and nuisance fitting induce dependence. We use Benjamini–Yekutieli (BY) as the primary FDR adjustment because its multiplicity result permits arbitrary dependence among valid null p-values. This does not remove the need for valid individual tests; here those are asymptotic approximations.

The calculation takes all 24 raw p-values, sorts them, and applies the BH step-up rule at 0.05/H_{24}, where H_{24}\approx3.78. It then maps the decisions back to feature IDs. This is the BY algorithm in Multiple testing when the tests are not independent; selecting just the displayed or smallest-p-value features first would change the family and the guarantee. Each null concerns the standardized contrast, so a gene with varying conditional contrasts can still be a true null in this family.

With a fixed family and jointly adequate marginal approximations, the argument supports asymptotic FDR control at 0.05. It is not an exact finite-sample guarantee. BH can be less conservative under appropriate dependence conditions, but positive feature correlations alone do not establish the required condition for these two-sided tests. See the Multiple testing when the tests are not independent page and R’s adjustment documentation.

9 Challenge the analysis in repeated samples

Within each replicate, coverage is the fraction of the 24 fixed targets covered, and FDP is false rejections divided by all rejections (zero when no tests reject). We average those replicate summaries. Their MCSEs use variation across independent datasets; genes within a dataset are not independent simulation replicates. A plug-in MCSE can be zero if every observed FDP is zero; that does not prove true FDR is zero.

Each attempt is retained with seeds, success status, and any error. Metrics are conditional on successful complete-family fits, so report success counts alongside them; do not interpret conditional performance as unconditional reliability when fits fail. One hundred replicates make this a runnable teaching experiment, with visibly reported Monte Carlo uncertainty. A research benchmark should plan its replicate count from the precision needed.

10 Reproduce the inputs, run, and report

Download the complete project, which includes the R functions, editable configuration, runner, observed synthetic assay/metadata, truth stored separately for evaluation, diagnostics, contributions, all simulation attempts, manifests, software information, Quarto report source, and rendered report. Analysis needs base R; rendering the report additionally needs a working Quarto R/knitr installation. The report reads explicit result files and has freezing disabled. We standardized group contrasts to the source-population covariate distribution using all people. For gene_01, the cross-fitted augmented estimate was 0.670 log2 units (SE 0.050; pointwise approximate 95% interval 0.572 to 0.767). The prespecified family contained 24 features; BY at 0.05 rejected 6 nulls. Fitted probabilities and means required clipping for 0.0% and 0.0% of their predictions. These are standardized associations under the stated MAR, overlap, sampling, and nuisance-rate conditions; they do not establish causal or absolute-abundance effects.

The scientific interpretation is bounded by the target and assumptions, even when execution is reproducible and the synthetic answer is close to truth. Return to Reporting a statistical methods study for the full claim/evidence checklist.

## One research argument, from measurements to a reported contrast

We will analyze 24 synthetic expression features from independently sampled people. Group membership is associated with a recorded covariate and technical batch. Some whole-assay measurements are missing. The question is: **how do the groups differ on the supplied log2 expression scale after standardizing to a common covariate distribution?**

This extended worked application follows one argument: define the target, audit the data, construct its estimator and uncertainty, then assess the reported family of claims. Simulation checks specific predictions of that argument. The [From an influence function to an estimator](../semiparametric-methods/influence-function-to-estimator.qmd) page supplies the proof pattern; here we combine group standardization with missing outcomes.

**Everything in this example is synthetic.** Feature IDs are invented. The generator supplies known truth for evaluation, while the estimator receives only the observed assay and metadata. No clinical or biological finding is being asserted.

## Define the population, scale, and target {#target-specification}

Let $A$ be group (0 or 1), $C=(Z,B)$ the recorded covariate and batch, and $Y_g$ a feature's calibrated log2 intensity. Define

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mu_{ag}(c)&=E[Y_g\mid A=a,C=c],\\
\psi_g&=E_C[\mu_{1g}(C)-\mu_{0g}(C)].
\end{aligned}
$$

The outer distribution is that of **all eligible people in the source population**, estimated using everyone's recorded $C$, including people with missing assays. We do not condition on the realized covariate values as a fixed population. Variation in the sampled covariate mix therefore contributes to the influence function.

::: {.table-responsive}

| Decision | Specification in this case |
|---|---|
| Independent unit | One person, one specimen, one assay occasion |
| Population | The source population sampled by the synthetic generator |
| Measurement | Supplied calibrated log2 intensity; no learned normalization |
| Contrast | Mean conditional group difference standardized over the source population's $C$ distribution |
| Missingness | One recorded indicator $R$ for a missing whole assay; all covariates remain observed |
| Family | All 24 prespecified feature hypotheses $H_{0g}:\psi_g=0$ |
| Interpretation | A standardized association; no causal interpretation is assumed |

:::

A log2 contrast exponentiates to a ratio of geometric means of the two standardized intensity distributions. It is not a ratio of arithmetic mean expression or an absolute molecules-per-cell contrast. The [Preprocessing and the quantity being estimated](../data-and-measurement/preprocessing-and-estimands.qmd) page explains those distinctions. Real count data would need their own measurement and normalization argument before this analysis could be reused.

Group contrasts can vary with $C$. We do not impose a constant coefficient or a common regression slope across groups. Nor does adjustment alone turn this association into an intervention effect. A causal interpretation would need a well-defined intervention and additional identification assumptions.

## Reconstruct the observation process

We observe $W=(C,A,R,RY_1,\ldots,RY_G)$. Algebraically $RY_g$ contributes zero when $R=0$; the stored assay uses `NA` for those outcomes. Treating those outcomes as measured zeros would change the analysis.

Assume $E[|Y_g|]<\infty$, outcome missingness at random given $(A,C)$, and positive group and observation probabilities on the target support:

$$
\begin{aligned}
e(c)&=P(A=1\mid C=c),\\
\pi_a(c)&=P(R=1\mid A=a,C=c),\\
q_a(c)&=P(A=a,R=1\mid C=c).
\end{aligned}
$$

Thus $q_1=e\pi_1$ and $q_0=(1-e)\pi_0$. MAR identifies $\mu_{ag}$ from the observed outcomes within group $a$. Positive $q_a$ provides data for each required group/covariate combination. Stronger lower bounds will support the simple variance and remainder argument below.

The missingness assumption is known in this generator. In real assays, failure to detect a low-abundance feature can depend on its unobserved value; that mechanism is not automatically MAR. Feature-specific non-detection, missing covariates, repeated specimens, and perfect group–batch confounding each require a revised analysis. See [Missingness, selection, and the observed-data target](../questions-and-design/missingness-and-selection.qmd) and [Units of analysis, replication, and study design](../questions-and-design/units-and-study-design.qmd).

## Generate and audit the study

The complete [R functions](../examples/adjusted-expression.R) use base R. Start from a fresh R session; each random operation has an explicit seed.

```{r}
source("../examples/adjusted-expression.R")

  people = length(unique(d$id)))

```

The assay has features in rows and person IDs in columns. Metadata are intentionally shuffled. `AlignExpressionStudy()` checks unique IDs, an exact match of specimen sets, and explicit column alignment. It rejects nonfinite observed outcomes and nonmissing values on rows declared unobserved. No complete-case sample exclusion is used to define the covariate distribution. Every person remains in the analysis.

The generator uses $Z\sim\operatorname{Uniform}(-1,1)$ and $B\sim\operatorname{Bernoulli}(1/2)$ independently, with

$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{logit}e(C)&=-0.1+Z+0.5B,\\
\operatorname{logit}\pi_A(C)&=0.6-0.6A +0.5Z-0.3B.
\end{aligned}
$$

Here $\operatorname{logit}(p)=\log\{p/(1-p)\}$; applying its inverse expit turns each linear predictor into a probability. This is a convenient factorization of the joint distribution, not a claim that technical batch causes group status. The conditional outcome mean is

$$
\mu_{ag}(Z,B)=b_g+c_gZ+d_g(Z^2-1/3) +t_gB+a(\delta_g+h_gZ).
$$

A shared bounded person-level noise component and independent bounded feature noise induce correlated features. Their conditional means are zero. Since $E[Z]=0$, the population standardized contrast is $\psi_g=\delta_g$. The first six features have nonzero $\delta_g$; the remaining eighteen have zero standardized contrasts, even though their conditional group contrasts vary with $Z$.

The generator returns these oracle values in a separate `truth` table. `EstimateExpressionContrast()` has no truth argument and does not receive the complete missing outcomes.

## Derive the contribution before fitting a model

The influence function in the unrestricted observed-data model, under identification and square integrability, is

$$
\phi_g(W)=\mu_{1g}(C)-\mu_{0g}(C)-\psi_g +\frac{AR}{q_1(C)}\{Y_g-\mu_{1g}(C)\} -\frac{(1-A)R}{q_0(C)}\{Y_g-\mu_{0g}(C)\}.
$$

The first line captures variation in the population's covariate mix. The second uses observed group-1 residuals; the third subtracts observed group-0 residuals. Conditional centering gives mean zero. In an inner product with an outcome-law score, the joint observation weight cancels the probability of observing that group's outcome. Scores changing group assignment or observation alone have zero derivative for this functional. Covariate-distribution scores pair with the first line. This follows the derivation in [From an influence function to an estimator](../semiparametric-methods/influence-function-to-estimator.qmd#derive-the-observed-data-influence-function) separately for the two standardized means.

The **uncentered contribution** starts with the two predicted means for everyone's $C$. An observed group-1 person then adds their weighted prediction error; an observed group-0 person subtracts theirs. Someone with a missing assay contributes only the predicted contrast, but remains part of the target covariate population. For example, suppose a held-out person's predictions are 6.5 and 6.0. If they are observed in group 1 with outcome 7.0 and fitted joint probability 0.25, their contribution is $0.5+(7.0-6.5)/0.25=2.5$. That is a weighted statistical contribution, not their measured or causal individual contrast.

Why the joint probability? Among people with the same $C$, only a fraction $q_1(C)$ both belong to group 1 **and** have an observed assay. Multiplying by $AR/q_1(C)$ makes that subgroup's residual correction represent its conditional population average. Weighting by the group probability alone would leave assay selection uncorrected; weighting by observation alone would leave the unequal group distribution of $C$ uncorrected.

For held-out person $i$, estimate all nuisances on other people, construct the uncentered contribution $\hat D_{ig}=\hat\phi_{ig}+\hat\psi_g$, and average it.

::: {#expression-crossfit-procedure .callout-note icon=false title="Algorithm: cross-fitted standardized expression contrast"}
**Inputs:** the aligned observed assay, all people's covariates and observation indicators, and prespecified fitting choices.

1. Randomly assign independent people to five folds. All features for a person stay together.
2. On each training set, fit group membership using `a ~ z + batch` and observation using <code>r ~ a + z + batch</code>.
3. Fit a quadratic outcome regression separately in each group, using only its observed training outcomes. All genes use the same design, but have separate coefficients.
4. Predict both group means, both observation probabilities, and the group probability on held-out covariates.
5. Add residual corrections only where the corresponding outcome is observed, then combine all held-out contributions.

**Output:** one contribution per person and feature, and their feature-wise means. Uncertainty additionally requires the remainder and sampling conditions below.
:::

The residual correction uses $q_a$, not just the group probability or just the assay observation probability. It never multiplies an `NA` residual by zero. In code, the group-1 update is:

```{r}
#| eval: false
observed_1 <- a == 1 & r == 1
score[observed_1, ] <- score[observed_1, , drop = FALSE] +
  sweep(y[observed_1, , drop = FALSE] - m1[observed_1, , drop = FALSE],
        1, q1[observed_1], "/")
```

This is the same code used in the downloadable function. For repeated specimens, splitting arbitrary columns would leak person-level information and the independent-person SE below would be inappropriate.

## Name the remainder and the inferential conditions {#case-remainder}

Hold one training fit fixed. Let $\Delta\mu_{ag}=\hat\mu_{ag}-\mu_{ag}$. Its exact population bias is

$$
P\hat D_g-\psi_g=E\!\left[\frac{\hat q_1-q_1}{\hat q_1}\Delta\mu_{1g}\right] -E\!\left[\frac{\hat q_0-q_0}{\hat q_0}\Delta\mu_{0g}\right].
$$

If each fitted joint probability is at least a fixed $\kappa>0$, its magnitude is bounded by

$$
\kappa^{-1}\big\{\|\hat q_1-q_1\|_2\|\Delta\mu_{1g}\|_2 +\|\hat q_0-q_0\|_2\|\Delta\mu_{0g}\|_2\big\}.
$$

Both correct outcome regressions can make this bias vanish with controlled, incorrect weights. Correct joint probabilities can do so with controlled, incorrect outcome regressions. These are **consistency** statements. They do not automatically justify the same estimated influence-function SE under misspecification.

For the efficient first-order argument used here, require i.i.d. people; fixed fold count with nonvanishing fold proportions; MAR; true and fitted joint probabilities bounded below; bounded outcomes and fitted means; L2 consistency of all nuisances; each displayed product $o_p(n^{-1/2})$; and finite nonzero influence variance. The fold-wise conditional proof in [From an influence function to an estimator](../semiparametric-methods/influence-function-to-estimator.qmd#checkpoint-sp-12) controls the empirical remainder. The bias bound controls the other remainder. Then

$$
\begin{aligned}
\hat\psi_g-\psi_g&=(P_n-P)\phi_g+o_p(n^{-1/2}),\\
\widehat{\operatorname{SE}}_g&=\frac{\operatorname{SD}(\hat D_{ig}-\hat\psi_g)}{\sqrt n}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The default generator and correctly specified finite-dimensional fits provide a transparent setting for these conditions. This does not demonstrate that those nuisance models are adequate in a real cohort. Nor does the fixed-24-feature argument establish uniform validity when the number of features grows rapidly with sample size.

The SE formula uses variation in the **constructed contributions across all sampled people**, including those with missing assays. It is not the raw expression SD divided by the square root of the observed assay count. Cross-fitting contributions share training fits; their dependence becomes negligible at first order only through the displayed expansion. The proof, rather than treating fitted scores as new independent data, justifies this variance calculation.

## Address the family of claims

Use the two-sided asymptotic Wald p-value for $H_{0g}:\psi_g=0$. All 24 tests belong to the prespecified family. Shared noise and nuisance fitting induce dependence. We use **Benjamini–Yekutieli (BY)** as the primary FDR adjustment because its multiplicity result permits arbitrary dependence among valid null p-values. This does not remove the need for valid individual tests; here those are asymptotic approximations.

The calculation takes all 24 raw p-values, sorts them, and applies the BH step-up rule at $0.05/H_{24}$, where $H_{24}\approx3.78$. It then maps the decisions back to feature IDs. This is the BY algorithm in [Multiple testing when the tests are not independent](multiple-testing.qmd#by-procedure); selecting just the displayed or smallest-p-value features first would change the family and the guarantee. Each null concerns the standardized contrast, so a gene with varying conditional contrasts can still be a true null in this family.

With a fixed family and jointly adequate marginal approximations, the argument supports asymptotic FDR control at 0.05. It is not an exact finite-sample guarantee. BH can be less conservative under appropriate dependence conditions, but positive feature correlations alone do not establish the required condition for these two-sided tests. See the [Multiple testing when the tests are not independent](multiple-testing.qmd) page and [R's adjustment documentation](https://stat.ethz.ch/R-manual/R-devel/library/stats/html/p.adjust.html).

Within each replicate, coverage is the fraction of the 24 fixed targets covered, and FDP is false rejections divided by all rejections (zero when no tests reject). We average those replicate summaries. Their MCSEs use variation across independent datasets; **genes within a dataset are not independent simulation replicates**. A plug-in MCSE can be zero if every observed FDP is zero; that does not prove true FDR is zero.

Each attempt is retained with seeds, success status, and any error. Metrics are conditional on successful complete-family fits, so report success counts alongside them; do not interpret conditional performance as unconditional reliability when fits fail. One hundred replicates make this a runnable teaching experiment, with visibly reported Monte Carlo uncertainty. A research benchmark should plan its replicate count from the precision needed.

## Reproduce the inputs, run, and report {#case-reproduction}

Download the [complete project](../examples/adjusted-expression-project.zip), which includes the R functions, editable configuration, runner, observed synthetic assay/metadata, truth stored separately for evaluation, diagnostics, contributions, all simulation attempts, manifests, software information, Quarto report source, and rendered report. Analysis needs base R; rendering the report additionally needs a working Quarto R/knitr installation. The report reads explicit result files and has freezing disabled. It can be regenerated without an interactive workspace.

## Exercises

Try each checkpoint before opening its hint or solution.

::: {#checkpoint-case-01 .checkpoint}
### Checkpoint · CASE-01 — Specify and identify the expression contrast {.unnumbered}

**Task type:** Interpretation and derivation.

Write the independent observation, measurement scale, population distribution, estimand, and tested family for this case. Derive why the generator gives $\psi_g=\delta_g$. Explain how a feature can have $\psi_g=0$ with conditional contrasts that vary with $Z$, and state the missingness/support assumptions needed to identify the full-population contrast.

**Ready to continue when:** you can distinguish the standardized target from a complete-case crude contrast, a constant regression coefficient, and a causal effect.

**Transfer:** If the target covariate distribution were that of group 1, would the same delta remain the target? Explain what changes in estimation and uncertainty.

<details class="checkpoint-help">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Average the conditional contrast over the specified population, then identify which outcome laws are actually observed.

</details>

<details class="checkpoint-help">
<summary>Solution outline — open after trying</summary>

One independent observation is a person’s covariates, group, observation indicator, and observed assay vector. The outcome is calibrated log2 intensity, and the family contains all 24 feature-wise standardized-contrast nulls. The conditional contrast is $\delta_g+h_gZ$; since the population has $E[Z]=0$, its average is $\delta_g$. A zero average need not imply zero contrast at every covariate value. MAR within group/covariates identifies the outcome regressions from observed outcomes; positive group and observation probabilities are needed throughout the target support, and covariates must be observed for the target population. None of these statements alone gives a causal or absolute-abundance interpretation.

</details>
:::

::: {#checkpoint-case-02 .checkpoint}
### Checkpoint · CASE-02 — Reconstruct the estimator and its remainder {.unnumbered}

**Task type:** Derivation and implementation.

Derive the conditional expectation of each residual correction, then the two-term exact bias displayed in this page. Explain why the weight uses the joint probability $q_a$. Reconstruct the fold procedure without accessing simulation truth or unobserved outcomes, and identify the conditions needed before using the estimated influence-function SE.

**Ready to continue when:** you can connect every correction and condition to an observed-data quantity and explain what double-robust consistency does and does not establish.

**Transfer:** If observation probabilities depend on the unobserved expression even after conditioning on group and $C$, which step of the argument fails?

<details class="checkpoint-help">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Hold a training fit fixed. For group a, the expected observed residual is q_a times the difference between the true and fitted conditional mean.

</details>

<details class="checkpoint-help">
<summary>Solution outline — open after trying</summary>

The group-a correction has fresh-observation expectation $E[q_a(\mu_{ag}-\hat\mu_{ag})/\hat q_a]$. Combining it with each plug-in mean yields the displayed signed product remainders. Weighting only by group or observation probability fails to cancel the joint observation process. Fit all nuisance functions on other people, evaluate both group predictions on all held-out covariates, and correct residuals only on observed rows of the matching group. For efficient inference, check nuisance consistency, lower probability bounds, product little-o rates, the held-out empirical term, finite variance and sampling/moment conditions. A wrong but controlled nuisance can permit consistency while invalidating that efficient-SE argument.

</details>
:::

::: {#checkpoint-case-03 .checkpoint}
### Checkpoint · CASE-03 — Interpret discoveries and uncertainty correctly {.unnumbered}

**Task type:** Interpretation and computation.

Explain the difference between a pointwise 95% interval, BY FDR control for the 24-feature family, a realized FDP in one simulation, and MCSE for estimated FDR. State the assumptions left after selecting BY and explain why you cannot treat 24 genes in each of 100 datasets as 2,400 independent replicates.

**Ready to continue when:** you can label each uncertainty quantity and error criterion, including the conditions multiplicity adjustment cannot repair.

**Transfer:** If you display only the twelve prespecified plot features, does the tested family shrink to twelve? What if those twelve were chosen after inspecting significance?

<details class="checkpoint-help">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Ask whether the expectation is over people, repeated datasets, or the feature family.

</details>

<details class="checkpoint-help">
<summary>Solution outline — open after trying</summary>

A pointwise interval targets one feature’s population contrast across samples. BY targets the expected FDP across repeated analyses of a stated family, allowing arbitrary p-value dependence but requiring valid null p-values; the Wald p-values here are asymptotically justified under the estimator’s conditions. One simulated FDP is known only because truth is supplied. Its average estimates FDR; its MCSE is the SD of replicate FDPs divided by the square root of the number of independent successful datasets. Within-dataset genes share people, noise, and fits. BY does not create simultaneous confidence intervals or repair missingness, target, or sampling errors.

</details>
:::

::: {#checkpoint-case-04 .checkpoint}
### Checkpoint · CASE-04 — Reproduce and challenge the complete analysis {.unnumbered}

**Task type:** Execution and diagnosis.

Unpack the project, regenerate it in a new results directory, render its report, and compare target estimates and simulation records with the reference run. Audit the input alignment, missing-outcome conventions, seeds, code/configuration identities, warnings, clipping, and attempted/successful counts. Before examining the four scenario summaries, predict which cases support consistency and which support the efficient-SE theorem.

**Ready to continue when:** you can rebuild the result, trace a claim to its inputs and assumptions, and diagnose a failed condition without silently discarding failures.

**Transfer:** Change only the number of folds or one model class, record a new run, and distinguish stochastic changes from a changed target or invalid theorem.

<details class="checkpoint-help">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use a fresh session and retain the reference directory. Derive the exact bias prediction before looking at the simulation plot.

</details>

<details class="checkpoint-help">
<summary>Solution outline — open after trying</summary>

The documented runner sources explicit functions and configuration, writes observed inputs separately from oracle truth, and records results, diagnostics, attempts, manifests, and software information. With the tested environment and seeds the statistical outputs reproduce; session metadata can differ across environments. Correct outcomes or correct joint weights can remove population bias under the stated controls. The fully consistent, sufficiently fast nuisance case supports the efficient-SE argument; single-model correctness alone does not. Both wrong leaves a generally nonzero remainder. Inspect failures and their denominators rather than assuming missing rows succeeded.

</details>
:::

### Additional practice (optional) {.unnumbered}

1. Extend the generator to feature-specific missingness and derive the corresponding observation probabilities; do not silently reuse the whole-assay indicator.
2. Use an external target covariate sample. Derive the new target and account for its sampling variation instead of treating the current influence function as unchanged.
3. Compare the four scenarios across more sample sizes and choose replicate counts from a prespecified MCSE goal. Treat singly correct nuisance cases as a separate inference problem.
4. Construct a simultaneous interval procedure for the prespecified family and explain how its claim differs from the plotted pointwise intervals.
