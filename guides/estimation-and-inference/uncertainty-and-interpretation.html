Turn a justified sampling approximation into a defensible claim

Before reading: Large-sample reasoning for methods research and Estimating equations and asymptotic linearity.

1 What a standard error describes

Continue with a completely observed feature mean \mu=E[Y], estimated by \hat\mu=\bar Y from n independent people. Let \sigma^2=\operatorname{Var}(Y). The population SD \sigma describes variation between people. The standard error \sigma/\sqrt n describes the spread of sample means across repeated studies of this size. Estimate it by s_Y/\sqrt n, where s_Y is the sample SD.

For example, a study of 100 people might have mean 2.5 and SD 2. Its estimated SE is 2/\sqrt{100}=0.2. The measurements vary on the scale of 2 units; the estimated mean’s sampling uncertainty is on the scale of 0.2 units. A confidence interval for the population mean will use the latter. Predicting a new person’s measurement requires a different calculation that includes individual variability.

The inferential starting point is the standardized error

\frac{\hat\mu-\mu}{\widehat{\operatorname{SE}}(\hat\mu)} \xrightarrow{d}N(0,1).

The numerator measures estimation error in outcome units; dividing by the estimated SE expresses it in units of sampling uncertainty. This is called studentization. The CLT, variance consistency, and Slutsky’s theorem justify it for the i.i.d. finite-variance mean.

The same construction applies to a more general scalar target \theta_0 when \hat\theta-\theta_0=P_nD_0+o_p(n^{-1/2}), PD_0=0, and 0<PD_0^2<\infty, with a consistently estimated contribution variance. The sampling unit, target, centering, and remainder conditions remain part of the argument.

2 A confidence interval is a random procedure

For a standard normal variable Z, \Pr(-1.96\le Z\le1.96)\approx0.95. Apply this reference to the standardized estimation error:

\Pr\!\left( \frac{|\hat\mu-\mu|}{\widehat{\operatorname{SE}}(\hat\mu)} \le1.96\right)\approx0.95.

Rearranging the inequality for the unknown \mu produces the approximate 95% Wald interval:

\left[\hat\mu-1.96\widehat{\operatorname{SE}},\; \hat\mu+1.96\widehat{\operatorname{SE}}\right].

For the example above, its margin is 1.96(0.2)=0.392, giving [2.108,2.892]. The point estimate and both endpoints would change if we collected another sample. The population mean would remain fixed.

Coverage is the probability that this random interval procedure includes the fixed population mean. Under the approximation’s conditions, about 95% of intervals from repeated studies cover it. In 1,000 simulated studies, the number covering need not be exactly 950. Once a particular dataset is observed, its endpoints are fixed; the frequentist coverage statement does not assign a 95% posterior probability to that realized interval containing \mu.

For independent normal measurements, replacing 1.96 by the appropriate t_{n-1} quantile gives an exact t interval. For more general asymptotically linear estimators, normal calibration is a large-sample approximation whose accuracy must be assessed for the setting.

A narrow interval can be centered at the wrong population quantity. If the observed-outcome mean targets 0.8 while the eligible-population mean is 2, a correct SE around 0.8 does not turn it into inference about 2. As the SE shrinks around the wrong limit, coverage for the intended target can deteriorate. Identification and target alignment precede interval calibration.

3 A test asks about a stated null

Specify the null before calculating a p-value. For the mean, take the point null H_0:\mu=\mu_\star. Under that null, the standardized statistic has an approximately standard normal distribution:

Z_n=\frac{\hat\mu-\mu_\star}{\widehat{\operatorname{SE}}}.

Let z_{\mathrm{obs}} be the statistic’s observed value. A two-sided p-value is the probability, under the null reference law, of a statistic at least this far from zero in either direction:

\begin{aligned} p&\approx\Pr(|Z|\ge|z_{\mathrm{obs}}|)\\ &=2\{1-\Phi(|z_{\mathrm{obs}}|)\},\\ Z&\sim N(0,1). \end{aligned}

Here \Phi(t)=\Pr(Z\le t) is the standard normal CDF. In the numerical example, testing \mu_\star=2 gives z_{\mathrm{obs}}=(2.5-2)/0.2=2.5 and p\approx0.0124. The observed mean is 2.5 estimated SEs above the null value. About 1.24% of a standard normal reference distribution lies at least that far from zero in absolute value.

With valid calibration, rejecting when p<0.05 gives a rejection probability of approximately 0.05 when the null is true. This is a repeated-sampling Type I error statement. It is not the probability that this particular null is true or that this particular finding is a false positive. The estimated difference, 0.5 outcome units here, describes magnitude; its practical importance requires scientific context.

For the same two-sided Wald procedure, a 95% interval excludes a null value exactly when its p-value is below 0.05, apart from rounding. Both decisions compare the distance from the estimate to the null with 1.96\widehat{\operatorname{SE}}. The example’s interval excludes 2, agreeing with its p-value. This agreement requires matched procedures; an arbitrary interval and test need not agree.

A non-rejection can reflect limited precision even when a scientifically relevant difference exists. To assess which differences the data support, inspect the estimate and interval. Establishing equivalence requires a specified equivalence margin and a corresponding inferential procedure.

4 See coverage across repeated samples

This synthetic experiment draws complete normal measurements with mean 2 and SD 1. It uses the approximate Wald rule with an estimated SE. It evaluates one prespecified mean, rather than a family of selected features.

Each replication draws a fresh group of 40 people, calculates its own mean and SE, and builds its own interval. The indicator covered records whether that interval contains the known \mu=2. In this matched example, noncoverage is also rejection of the true point null; it is not a power calculation under an alternative.

Code 
set.seed(812)
n <- 40
replicates <- 500
means <- ses <- numeric(replicates)
for (b in seq_len(replicates)) {
  y <- rnorm(n, mean = 2, sd = 1)
  means[b] <- mean(y)
  ses[b] <- sd(y) / sqrt(n)
}
covered <- abs(means - 2) <= 1.96 * ses
c(coverage = mean(covered),
  rejection_under_null = mean(!covered),
  coverage_MCSE = sqrt(mean(covered) * (1 - mean(covered)) / replicates))
            coverage rejection_under_null        coverage_MCSE 
         0.950000000          0.050000000          0.009746794
Code 
shown <- seq_len(80)
plot(means[shown], shown, xlim = range(means[shown] + 1.96 * ses[shown],
     means[shown] - 1.96 * ses[shown]), xlab = "Feature mean", ylab = "Independent sample",
     pch = 16, col = ifelse(covered[shown], "#2F80C8", "#D55E00"))
segments(means[shown] - 1.96 * ses[shown], shown,
         means[shown] + 1.96 * ses[shown], shown,
         col = ifelse(covered[shown], "#2F80C8", "#D55E00"))
abline(v = 2, lty = 2)

Coverage in a finite simulation fluctuates. With 500 replications and coverage near 0.95, the Monte Carlo SE is about \sqrt{0.95(0.05)/500}\approx0.01, or one percentage point. More simulated datasets improve the precision of this evaluation; increasing the number of people in each dataset changes the inferential problem itself. The plot shows 80 intervals for readability, while the numerical summary uses all 500.

One interval containing the truth is not evidence that a procedure has 95% coverage, and one miss is not evidence that it fails. Repeated-sampling evaluation studies the procedure. Its assumptions and asymptotic argument explain when we expect that evaluation to transfer beyond this generator.

5 Individual uncertainty and a family of claims

A pointwise interval concerns one prespecified target. Searching many genes, selecting the smallest p-values, and reporting those intervals is a different procedure. A collection of pointwise 95% intervals is not automatically a simultaneous confidence statement or a false-discovery guarantee. Multiple testing when the tests are not independent handles the family-level question after the individual inferential argument has been established.

Transfer: Would the same SE apply if the 100 rows were five visits from each of 20 people?

Hint

The SD of the observations is 2. Divide by the square root of the independent sample size to estimate the mean’s SE.

Solution outline — open after trying

The SE is 0.2; the Wald interval is approximately [2.008, 2.792]. The null statistic is 2, giving p approximately 0.0455. The matched interval excludes 2.
